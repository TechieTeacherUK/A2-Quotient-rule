Differentiation: The Quotient Rule

Edexcel A-Level Mathematics (Pure, Year 2) · Learn it, see worked examples, then practise

How to use this lesson. Read each card, play with the graph in the last one, then try the worked examples before you practise.
Words you need (in plain English)
  • Differentiate: find the gradient function, a formula that tells you how steep the curve is at any point.
  • Derivative, written dy/dx: the answer you get when you differentiate. It is the gradient of the curve.
  • Quotient: one expression divided by another, like $\dfrac{e^{2x}}{x}$. We call the expression on top the top (numerator) and the one underneath the bottom (denominator).
  • Stationary point: a point where the curve is flat for a moment, so the gradient is $0$.
  • Common factor: something that appears in every term, so you can take it outside a bracket.
1. When do you need the quotient rule?

You need it when one expression containing $x$ is divided by another expression containing $x$. For example:

$y=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{x}$    $y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$    $y=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}$

You cannot differentiate the top and the bottom separately and divide the answers. The gradient of a fraction is not "gradient of the top divided by gradient of the bottom".

You do not always need it. If the bottom is just a number, like $\dfrac{x^2+1}{3}$, differentiate normally. If the bottom is a single power of $x$, you can often split the fraction first: $\dfrac{x^2+1}{x}=x+\dfrac{1}{x}$.

2. The rule

If $y=\dfrac{u}{v}$, where $u$ and $v$ are both expressions in $x$, then

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$$

Here $u'$ means "the derivative of $u$" and $v'$ means "the derivative of $v$".

In words: bottom times the derivative of the top, minus top times the derivative of the bottom, all divided by the bottom squared.

The order matters. The minus sign means $u'v-uv'$ is not the same as $uv'-u'v$. Start with the derivative of the top.

Method:

  1. Pick $u$ (the top) and $v$ (the bottom).
  2. Differentiate each one to get $u'$ and $v'$.
  3. Put them into $\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$. Use brackets around everything.
  4. Tidy up the top. Take out any common factor.

Quick example: $y=\dfrac{x^2}{x+1}$. Here $u=x^2$, $v=x+1$, so $u'=2x$ and $v'=1$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{2x(x+1)-x^2(1)}{(x+1)^2}=\dfrac{2x^2+2x-x^2}{(x+1)^2}=\dfrac{x^2+2x}{(x+1)^2}=\dfrac{x(x+2)}{(x+1)^2}$

Notice that $v^2$ means the whole bottom squared. You do not differentiate it.

3. Derivatives you need to know
FunctionDerivative
$x^n$$nx^{n-1}$
$e^{kx}$$ke^{kx}$
$\ln x$$\dfrac{1}{x}$
$\sin x$$\cos x$
$\cos x$$-\sin x$ (watch the minus sign!)

Notice $e^{3x}$ gives $3e^{3x}$: the number in front of $x$ comes down as a multiplier.

4. Mistakes that lose marks
  • Dividing the derivatives: $\dfrac{u'}{v'}$ is wrong. You need $\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$.
  • Swapping the order on top: $uv'-u'v$ gives the answer with the wrong sign.
  • Forgetting to square the bottom: the bottom of the answer is $v^2$, not $v$.
  • Losing a minus sign: when you subtract $uv'$, put it in brackets so the minus applies to every term. For example $-(2x+1)=-2x-1$, not $-2x+1$.
  • Not simplifying: take out common factors, especially $e^{kx}$. It makes solving $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ much easier.
